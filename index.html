Explaining the history of the lightbulb's invention.

The incandescent light bulb, the kind most people picture 🤷💡🖼️, was not invented by one person. It came together over about 80 years, with many inventors contributing.

Early experiments (1800s)

In 1802, Humphry Davy showed that an electric current passed between two carbon rods 🪄💡🪄 produced light. This “arc lamp” was bright but too harsh and short-lived for homes. The principle that mattered for the bulb was that a thin conductor heated by current can glow. Several inventors in the 1840s and 1850s, including Warren de la Rue and Frederick de Moleyns, built glowing filaments in vacuum or gas-filled glass, but they were expensive or burned out quickly. The main obstacle was that a hot filament in air oxidizes and disintegrates almost at once, so the bulb 💡 had to be evacuated.

Swan and Edison (1878–1880)

Joseph Swan in England and Thomas Edison in the United States both developed working carbon-filament lamps 🛋️ around 1878–1879. Swan demonstrated his lamps publicly in Newcastle in 1879. Edison’s team at Menlo Park tested thousands of materials for filaments before finding carbonized cotton thread and later bamboo, which lasted hundreds of hours. Edison also built the rest of the system: generators, wiring, fixtures, and a central power station, Pearl Street in New York, which began service in 1882. The two men’s companies later merged in Britain as Ediswan. Patent disputes between them and others were long and tangled.

Improving the filament (1900s–1910s)

Carbon filaments were fragile and inefficient. Tungsten, with its very high melting point, became the standard. Around 1910, William Coolidge at General Electric developed a method to make tungsten ductile enough to draw into fine wire. Irving Langmuir, also at GE, found that filling bulbs with inert gas (argon, and later mixtures) instead of a vacuum slowed filament evaporation and allowed hotter, brighter operation. The tungsten-filled, gas-filled bulb is essentially the design still used in ordinary incandescent lamps today.

Planned obsolescence?

A 1924 agreement among major manufacturers, the so-called Phoebus cartel, is often cited as an effort to limit bulb life to about 1,000 hours. Historians debate 🤔 how much it actually affected product design, but it is a frequently told episode.

Alternatives

Fluorescent lamps became practical in the late 1930s, producing light by exciting phosphors with ultraviolet light from mercury vapor. They are far more efficient than incandescent bulbs.

Halogen lamps (1950s–1960s) add a halogen gas that redeposits evaporated tungsten back on the filament, extending life and allowing brighter, smaller bulbs.

LEDs: Henry Round observed light from a semiconductor in 1907, but the first practical visible LEDs appeared in the early 1960s. Blue LEDs, which made white light practical, came in the 1990s through work by Isamu Akasaki, Hiroshi Amano, and Shuji Nakamura, who shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics for it. LEDs now dominate lighting because they use far less energy and last much longer.

Regulation

Many countries have phased out inefficient incandescent bulbs. The United States began tightening efficiency standards in 2007, and the European Union restricted incandescent sales in the late 2000s and 2010s.
